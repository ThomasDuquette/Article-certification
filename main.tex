\section{Introduction}

B-splines are widely used in robotic trajectory optimization due to their smoothness, compact parametric representation, and suitability for numerical optimization. These properties make them particularly attractive for generating trajectories for robotic manipulators and autonomous vehicles, where trajectories must satisfy kinematic constraints throughout their execution.

Verifying these constraints over a continuous trajectory remains challenging. In particular, determining the extrema of trajectory quantities, such as velocity and acceleration, is essential to establish whether prescribed bounds are satisfied at every instant. Exploiting the piecewise-polynomial structure of B-splines makes it possible to formulate extremum finding as a root-finding problem. However, solving these problems efficiently and reliably remains a challenge, particularly when repeated evaluations are required during trajectory optimization or certification.

Existing approaches commonly rely on the convex-hull property of B-splines or finite trajectory discretization to assess constraint satisfaction. Although convex-hull-based methods provide sufficient conditions for feasibility, they can be conservative and reject trajectories that satisfy the constraints. Discretization-based methods, in contrast, can overlook violations occurring between samples and may require dense sampling to achieve adequate resolution. These limitations motivate methods that can determine trajectory extrema efficiently while providing rigorous guarantees on their results.

This article addresses the problem of finding extrema of B-spline trajectories by exploiting their piecewise-polynomial representation. We develop a computational pipeline incorporating a context-aware, bounded Newton algorithm and establish theoretical guarantees for the proposed method. The resulting approach aims to provide an efficient alternative to conventional trajectory verification techniques without relying solely on conservative bounds or dense discretization.

We evaluate the proposed method for B-spline trajectory certification in robotic manipulators using randomized and optimized trajectory datasets comprising over 30,000 distinct B-splines.

\subsection{Contributions}

The main contributions of this article are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We develop a pipeline for finding extrema of B-spline trajectories by exploiting their piecewise-polynomial structure.
    \item We introduce a context-aware, bounded Newton algorithm and formally establish its guarantees.
    \item We evaluate its accuracy and computational efficiency against the convex hull and the discretization methods. 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{B-spline Trajectories as Piecewise Polynomials}
\label{sec:bspline_pp}

Let $\mathbf{q}:[t_p,t_N]\to\mathbb{R}^J$ be a joint-space trajectory of $J$ joints, represented as a B-spline of degree $p$ with $N$ control points $\mathbf{c}_0,\dots,\mathbf{c}_{N-1}\in\mathbb{R}^J$ and a non-decreasing knot vector $t_0\le t_1\le\dots\le t_{N+p}$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{q}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbf{c}_n\,B_{n,p}(t).
  \label{eq:bspline}
\end{equation}
The basis functions follow the Cox--de Boor recursion,
\begin{align}
  B_{n,0}(t) &=
  \begin{cases}
    1 & t\in[t_n,t_{n+1}),\\
    0 & \text{otherwise,}
  \end{cases}\\
  B_{n,r}(t) &= \frac{t-t_n}{t_{n+r}-t_n}\,B_{n,r-1}(t)
              + \frac{t_{n+r+1}-t}{t_{n+r+1}-t_{n+1}}\,B_{n+1,r-1}(t),
\end{align}
with the convention that terms with a zero denominator vanish. In practice, such degenerate knot spans are empty and do not have incidence on the trajectory. The basis functions are non-negative, sum to one, and $B_{n,p}$ has compact support $[t_n,t_{n+p+1})$.

\paragraph*{Piecewise polynomial structure}
Within each knot span $[t_{i+p},t_{i+p+1})$, $i=0,\dots,m-1$ with $m=N-p$, only the $p+1$ basis functions $B_{i,p},\dots,B_{i+p,p}$ are non-zero, and each is a polynomial of degree $p$. Hence $\mathbf{q}$ restricted to span $i$ is a vector-valued polynomial depending on the local control points $\mathbf{c}_i,\dots,\mathbf{c}_{i+p}$ only. With the local parameter
\begin{equation}
  \tau=\frac{t-t_{i+p}}{\Delta_i}\in[0,1],\qquad \Delta_i=t_{i+p+1}-t_{i+p},
\end{equation}
this polynomial can be written in monomial form as
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{q}(t)=P_i(\tau)=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\mathbf{a}_{i,j}\,\tau^{j},
  \label{eq:local_poly}
\end{equation}
with coefficients given by
\begin{equation}
  A_i = M_i\,C_i,
  \label{eq:coeff_map}
\end{equation}
where $A_i=[\mathbf{a}_{i,0}\ \cdots\ \mathbf{a}_{i,p}]^{\top}$ and $C_i=[\mathbf{c}_i\ \cdots\ \mathbf{c}_{i+p}]^{\top}$ both lie in $\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times J}$, and the basis matrix $M_i\in\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times(p+1)}$ depends only on the knots $t_i,\dots,t_{i+2p+1}$.
where the basis matrix $M_i\in\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times(p+1)}$ depends only on the knots $t_i,\dots,t_{i+2p+1}$. The polynomial coefficients are therefore a fixed linear map of the control points, which are the decision variables of the trajectory optimization.

\paragraph*{Derivatives}
For $k\ge 0$, differentiating~\eqref{eq:local_poly} with respect to time gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{q}^{(k)}(t)=\Delta_i^{-k}\,P_i^{(k)}(\tau),
\end{equation}
where $P_i^{(k)}$ is a polynomial of degree $p-k$ in $\tau$. For uniform knots, $\Delta_i=T/m$ for total duration $T$, so the time scaling is the constant factor $(m/T)^k$. Since $B_{n,p}\in C^{p-1}$ at knots of multiplicity one, $\mathbf{q}$ and its derivatives up to order $p-1$ are continuous across span boundaries, while $\mathbf{q}^{(p)}$ is piecewise constant and generally discontinuous. In particular, $P_i^{(p-1)}$ is linear in $\tau$ on each span, a fact exploited in Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\paragraph*{Consequence for constraint evaluation}
Kinematic limits on $\mathbf{q}^{(k)}$, $k\le p-2$, require the extrema of each joint's $k$-th derivative over the whole trajectory. As $\mathbf{q}^{(k)}$ is continuous and piecewise polynomial, these extrema occur either at span boundaries or at interior roots of $P_i^{(k+1)}$ on $(0,1)$. Therefore, locating the roots of the local polynomials yields the extrema over the continuous domain.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Let $\mathbf{q}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^J$ be a joint-space trajectory represented by a B-spline of degree $p$ with $N$ control points and total duration $T$, as defined in Section~\ref{sec:bspline_pp}, and let $q_j$ denote its $j$-th joint component. Kinematic limits on velocity, acceleration, jerk, and higher-order derivatives are constraints of the form
\begin{equation}
  \underline{L}_{j,k}\;\le\; q_j^{(k)}(t)\;\le\;\overline{L}_{j,k}
  \qquad \forall\, t\in[0,T],
  \label{eq:limits}
\end{equation}
for joints $j=1,\dots,J$ and derivative orders $k=0,\dots,d$, where $d\le p-2$. Verifying~\eqref{eq:limits} requires the extrema of each derivative over the entire continuous time domain,
\begin{equation}
  q_{j,k}^{\min}=\min_{t\in[0,T]} q_j^{(k)}(t),
  \qquad
  q_{j,k}^{\max}=\max_{t\in[0,T]} q_j^{(k)}(t),
  \label{eq:extrema}
\end{equation}


\begin{problem}[Certified derivative extrema]
Given the control points $\mathbf{c}_0,\dots,\mathbf{c}_{N-1}$, the degree $p$, the knot vector, the duration $T$, a maximal derivative order $d\le p-2$, and a tolerance $\varepsilon>0$, compute for every joint $j$ and every order $k=0,\dots,d$ an enclosure $[\,\underline{b}_{j,k},\overline{b}_{j,k}\,]$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{(Soundness)} $\underline{b}_{j,k}\le q_{j,k}^{\min}$ and $q_{j,k}^{\max}\le\overline{b}_{j,k}$;
  \item \emph{(Tightness)} the overestimation $q_{j,k}^{\min}-\underline{b}_{j,k}$ and $\overline{b}_{j,k}-q_{j,k}^{\max}$ is controlled by $\varepsilon$ and vanishes as $\varepsilon\to 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

Soundness guarantees that no violation of~\eqref{eq:limits} is missed: if $\underline{L}_{j,k}\le\underline{b}_{j,k}$ and $\overline{b}_{j,k}\le\overline{L}_{j,k}$, the trajectory is certified feasible for all $t\in[0,T]$. Tightness limits false rejections of feasible trajectories. As $\mathbf{q}^{(k)}$ is piecewise polynomial of degree $p-k$ with $p-k\ge 2$ for $k\le d$, the problem reduces to finding, on each of the $m=N-p$ segments, the real roots of the polynomial $P_i^{(k+1)}$ of degree $p-k-1$ in $[0,1]$, which has no closed-form solution in general for $p-k-1\ge 5$. The method must also be fast enough to be evaluated repeatedly, for instance inside a trajectory optimization loop. In this work we address kinematic constraints on a single trajectory only.

\section{Proposed method} \label{sec:proposed_method}
% Requires: \usepackage{algorithm}, \usepackage{algpseudocode}, \usepackage{amsmath,amssymb}
% \iv{f}(X) denotes the interval extension of f over the interval X.
% Candidate times follow from the affine map t_i + \Delta_i * tau and are carried along with E_k.
% Segment endpoints are added for every segment (duplicates at shared knots are harmless).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Certified extrema of a B-spline and its derivatives}
\label{alg:cascade}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Segment polynomials $P_1,\dots,P_m$ of degree $p$ on $[0,1]$, order $d<p-1$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, duration $T$
\Ensure Interval enclosures $\mathcal{E}_k$ of all candidate extrema of $P^{(k)}$, $k=0,\dots,d$
\Statex
\Procedure{Refine}{$f,\mathcal{R}$} \Comment{$\mathcal{R}$ encloses the roots of $f'$}
  \State $\mathcal{R}' \gets \{\, r\in\mathcal{R} : 0\in\iv{f}(r) \,\}$ \Comment{keep enclosures that may hold a root}
  \For{each gap $[g,h]$ of $[0,1]\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{R}$} \Comment{$f$ monotone on $[g,h]$}
    \If{$f(g)\,f(h)\le 0$}
      \State $\mathcal{R}' \gets \mathcal{R}' \cup \{\textsc{SafeNewton}(f,g,h,\varepsilon)\}$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\mathcal{R}'$
\EndProcedure
\Statex
\State $\mathcal{E}_k \gets \emptyset$ for $k=0,\dots,d$
\For{$i=1,\dots,m$}
  \State $\mathcal{R} \gets \{\tau\in[0,1] : P_i^{(p-1)}(\tau)=0\}$ as point intervals \Comment{linear: exact}
  \For{$k=p-2,\dots,0$}
    \If{$k\le d$}
      \State $\mathcal{E}_k \gets \mathcal{E}_k \cup \{\square P_i^{(k)}(r) : r\in\mathcal{R}\} \cup \{P_i^{(k)}(0),\,P_i^{(k)}(1)\}$
    \EndIf
    \If{$k>0$} \State $\mathcal{R} \gets \textsc{Refine}(P_i^{(k)},\mathcal{R})$ \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $(m/T)^k\,\mathcal{E}_k$ for $k=0,\dots,d$ \Comment{time normalization}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{SafeNewton}$(f,\ell,u,\varepsilon)$}
\label{alg:safenewton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Polynomial $f$ with $f(\ell)f(u)\le 0$, tolerance $\varepsilon$
\Ensure $[\ell,u]$ with $u-\ell<\varepsilon$ and a sign change of $f$
\State $x \gets (\ell+u)/2$
\While{true}
  \If{$f(\ell)\,f(x)\le 0$} $u\gets x$ \Else{} $\ell\gets x$ \EndIf \Comment{shrink bracket}
  \If{$u-\ell<\varepsilon$} \Return $[\ell,u]$ \EndIf
  \State $x_N \gets x - f(x)/f'(x)$
  \State $x \gets x_N$ if $\ell<x_N<u$, \textbf{else} $(\ell+u)/2$ \Comment{bisection fallback}
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Soundness of the Cascade}
\label{sec:soundness}

% Refers to Algorithm~\ref{alg:cascade}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:safenewton}, Problem~1 and Sec.~\ref{sec:bspline_pp}.

\paragraph*{Notation}
Fix one joint and one segment, and write $P$ for its local polynomial on $[0,1]$ and $f_k=P^{(k)}$ for its $k$-th derivative, a polynomial of degree $p-k$. Let $Z_k=\{\tau\in[0,1]: f_k(\tau)=0\}$ denote its real roots in $[0,1]$. A finite set $\mathcal{R}$ of closed intervals contained in $[0,1]$ \emph{encloses} a set $Z\subseteq[0,1]$ if $Z\subseteq\bigcup\mathcal{R}$. Point intervals $[a,a]$ are allowed. Enclosure is a statement about coverage only: it carries no information on how many roots lie in each interval. We write $\iv{f}(X)$ for the interval evaluation of $f$ over an interval $X$.

\begin{assumption}[Interval evaluation]\label{as:ia}
For every polynomial $f$ and interval $X\subseteq[0,1]$, $f(\tau)\in\iv{f}(X)$ for all $\tau\in X$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Sign tests]\label{as:sign}
The signs of $f_k$ at the points used in \textsc{Refine} and \textsc{SafeNewton}, and of products of such values, are evaluated correctly.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Base case]\label{as:base}
The initial set $\mathcal{R}_{p-2}$ in Algorithm~\ref{alg:cascade} encloses $Z_{p-1}$. Since $f_{p-1}$ is linear, this holds with the point interval $[-a_0/a_1,\,-a_0/a_1]$ when $f_{p-1}(\tau)=a_1\tau+a_0$ with $a_1\neq0$ and the root lies in $[0,1]$. If $a_1=0\neq a_0$ then $Z_{p-1}=\emptyset$ and $\mathcal{R}_{p-2}=\emptyset$. If $f_{p-1}\equiv0$ we take $\mathcal{R}_{p-2}=\{[0,1]\}$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{lemma}[Reduction to segments]\label{lem:segments}
Let $0\le k\le p-1$, let the interior knots have multiplicity one, and let $\Delta_i=t_{i+p+1}-t_{i+p}$. Then the range of $q_j^{(k)}$ over $[t_p,t_N]$ equals the union over segments $i$ of the ranges of $\Delta_i^{-k}P_{i,j}^{(k)}$ over $[0,1]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
On the half-open span $[t_{i+p},t_{i+p+1})$ we have $q_j^{(k)}(t)=\Delta_i^{-k}P_{i,j}^{(k)}(\tau)$ with $\tau\in[0,1)$. At an interior knot, $q_j^{(k)}$ is continuous because $q_j\in C^{p-1}$, so its value is the left limit $\Delta_i^{-k}P_{i,j}^{(k)}(1)$. At $t_N$ the value is defined by the same continuity. Hence every value of $q_j^{(k)}$ is attained by some piece on $[0,1]$, and every value of every piece on the closed interval $[0,1]$, including $\tau=1$, is attained by $q_j^{(k)}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Candidate set]\label{lem:candidates}
Let $f$ be a polynomial and let $\mathcal{R}$ enclose the roots of $f'$ in $[0,1]$. Define the candidate set
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{C}(f,\mathcal{R})=\{\,[f(0),f(0)],\ [f(1),f(1)]\,\}\cup\{\,\iv{f}(r): r\in\mathcal{R}\,\}.
\end{equation}
Then $\min_{[0,1]}f$ and $\max_{[0,1]}f$ both belong to $\bigcup\mathcal{C}(f,\mathcal{R})$. Consequently, $f(\tau)\in[\inf\bigcup\mathcal{C},\ \sup\bigcup\mathcal{C}]$ for all $\tau\in[0,1]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$f$ is continuous on the compact set $[0,1]$, so it attains its minimum and maximum at some $\tau^\star$. If $\tau^\star\in\{0,1\}$, then $f(\tau^\star)$ is one of the first two members of $\mathcal{C}$. Otherwise $\tau^\star\in(0,1)$ is an interior extremum of a differentiable function, so $f'(\tau^\star)=0$ by Fermat's theorem. Then $\tau^\star$ lies in some $r\in\mathcal{R}$, and $f(\tau^\star)\in\iv{f}(r)$ by Assumption~\ref{as:ia}. The last claim follows because every value of $f$ lies between its minimum and maximum.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[\textsc{SafeNewton}]\label{lem:newton}
Let $f$ be continuous with $f(\ell_0)f(u_0)\le0$. Then, under Assumption~\ref{as:sign}, every interval $[\ell,u]$ held by Algorithm~\ref{alg:safenewton} after any update step satisfies $[\ell,u]\subseteq[\ell_0,u_0]$ and $f(\ell)f(u)\le0$. In particular, whenever the algorithm returns, $[\ell,u]$ contains a root of $f$ in $[\ell_0,u_0]$. This holds regardless of how the iteration $x$ is chosen, as long as $x\in[\ell,u]$, and independently of $\varepsilon$ and of any iteration cap.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We argue by induction on the updates. The invariant holds initially. In one update, either $f(\ell)f(x)\le0$ and we set $u\gets x$, which gives $f(\ell)f(u)=f(\ell)f(x)\le0$ directly. Or $f(\ell)f(x)>0$, so $f(\ell)\ne0$ and $f(x)$ has the same sign as $f(\ell)$, and we set $\ell\gets x$. Then $f(x)f(u)=\dfrac{f(x)}{f(\ell_{\mathrm{old}})}\,f(\ell_{\mathrm{old}})f(u)\le0$, because the ratio is positive and $f(\ell_{\mathrm{old}})f(u)\le0$ by the induction hypothesis. The point $x$ is either a Newton iterate accepted only if $\ell<x_N<u$, or the midpoint of $[\ell,u]$, so $x\in[\ell,u]$ and the bracket stays inside $[\ell_0,u_0]$. Finally, $f(\ell)f(u)\le0$ and continuity give a root in $[\ell,u]$ by the intermediate value theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[\textsc{Refine}]\label{lem:refine}
Let $f$ be a polynomial and let $\mathcal{R}$ be a finite set of closed intervals in $[0,1]$ enclosing the roots of $f'$ in $[0,1]$. Then, under Assumptions~\ref{as:ia} and~\ref{as:sign}, $\mathcal{R}'=\textsc{Refine}(f,\mathcal{R})$ is a finite set of closed intervals in $[0,1]$ enclosing the roots of $f$ in $[0,1]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Finiteness and the inclusion in $[0,1]$ hold because $\mathcal{R}'$ consists of members of $\mathcal{R}$ and of intervals returned by \textsc{SafeNewton} on subintervals of $[0,1]$. Let $U=\bigcup\mathcal{R}$ and let $\tau_0\in[0,1]$ satisfy $f(\tau_0)=0$.

\emph{Case 1: $\tau_0\in U$.} Then $\tau_0\in r$ for some $r\in\mathcal{R}$, and $0=f(\tau_0)\in\iv{f}(r)$ by Assumption~\ref{as:ia}. So $r$ passes the test $0\in\iv{f}(r)$ and is kept in $\mathcal{R}'$.

\emph{Case 2: $\tau_0\notin U$.} The set $U$ is closed, so $[0,1]\setminus U$ is relatively open in $[0,1]$ and is a finite union of disjoint intervals. Let $\Gamma$ be the component containing $\tau_0$ and $[g,h]$ its closure, which equals the gap examined by \textsc{Refine}, with $g<h$. Since $\Gamma\cap U=\emptyset$, $f'$ has no root in the interior $(g,h)$. By continuity $f'$ has constant strict sign there, so $f$ is strictly monotone on $[g,h]$ by the mean value theorem. As $f(\tau_0)=0$ with $\tau_0\in[g,h]$, strict monotonicity gives that $f(g)$ and $f(h)$ do not have the same strict sign, so $f(g)f(h)\le0$. Hence the test of \textsc{Refine} passes and $\textsc{SafeNewton}(f,g,h,\varepsilon)$ is called. By Lemma~\ref{lem:newton} it returns an interval containing a root of $f$ in $[g,h]$. Strict monotonicity on $[g,h]$ means $\tau_0$ is the only such root, so the returned interval contains $\tau_0$.

In both cases $\tau_0\in\bigcup\mathcal{R}'$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Soundness of the cascade]\label{thm:soundness}
Let Assumptions~\ref{as:ia}--\ref{as:base} hold and let $0\le d\le p-2$. For each joint $j$, derivative order $k\le d$ and segment $i$, let $\mathcal{R}^{(i)}_k$ be the set $\mathcal{R}$ at the start of iteration $k$ of Algorithm~\ref{alg:cascade}, and let $\mathcal{E}_k$ be the output for order $k$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\mathcal{R}^{(i)}_k$ encloses $Z_{k+1}$ for every $k\in\{p-2,\dots,0\}$;
  \item for every $t\in[t_p,t_N]$, $q_j^{(k)}(t)\in\big[\inf\bigcup\mathcal{E}_k,\ \sup\bigcup\mathcal{E}_k\big]$, in particular $\underline{b}_{j,k}\le q_{j,k}^{\min}$ and $q_{j,k}^{\max}\le\overline{b}_{j,k}$, with $\underline{b}_{j,k}=\inf\bigcup\mathcal{E}_k$ and $\overline{b}_{j,k}=\sup\bigcup\mathcal{E}_k$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix a segment and a joint, and drop the indices $i,j$.

\emph{(1) Invariant, by downward induction on $k$.} For $k=p-2$, the set $\mathcal{R}$ is the set from Assumption~\ref{as:base}, which encloses $Z_{p-1}=Z_{k+1}$. Suppose $\mathcal{R}_k$ encloses $Z_{k+1}$ for some $k\ge1$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:cascade} sets $\mathcal{R}_{k-1}=\textsc{Refine}(f_k,\mathcal{R}_k)$. Since $f_k'=f_{k+1}$, Lemma~\ref{lem:refine} applies and shows that $\mathcal{R}_{k-1}$ encloses $Z_k=Z_{(k-1)+1}$.

\emph{(2) Segment bounds.} For $k\le d$, the algorithm adds to $\mathcal{E}_k$ the set $\mathcal{C}(f_k,\mathcal{R}_k)$ multiplied by the factor $(m/T)^k$. Since $\mathcal{R}_k$ encloses the roots of $f_k'=f_{k+1}$ by (1), Lemma~\ref{lem:candidates} gives that $f_k(\tau)\in[\inf\bigcup\mathcal{C},\sup\bigcup\mathcal{C}]$ for all $\tau\in[0,1]$. For uniform knots $\Delta_i=T/m$, so $(m/T)^k=\Delta_i^{-k}>0$ and multiplication by it preserves enclosure and order.

\emph{(3) Combination.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:segments}, every value of $q_j^{(k)}$ on $[t_p,t_N]$ is a value of $\Delta^{-k}P_{i,j}^{(k)}$ on $[0,1]$ for some segment $i$. By (2) it lies in the interval spanned by the candidates of that segment, and hence in the interval spanned by the union over all segments, which is $[\inf\bigcup\mathcal{E}_k,\sup\bigcup\mathcal{E}_k]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What is used about retained intervals]
The proof only uses coverage. An interval retained in \textsc{Refine} because $0\in\iv{f}(r)$ may contain zero, one, or several roots of $f$. Passing it unchanged to the next level is sound, and the rare event in which this happens affects computation time but not the validity of the result.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Role of $\varepsilon$ and of the iteration cap]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:newton}, soundness does not depend on $\varepsilon$ nor on whether \textsc{SafeNewton} exits at the width test or at an iteration cap. Tightness and running time do depend on them and are not covered by Theorem~\ref{thm:soundness}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Floating-point arithmetic]
The proof is stated for exact sign evaluation and an outward-rounded interval evaluator. In an implementation, Assumption~\ref{as:sign} holds if the sign tests in \textsc{Refine} and \textsc{SafeNewton} are certified, for instance by deciding the sign of $f(x)$ from the interval $\iv{f}{X}$ and treating undecided cases as sign changes, and if the base root $-a_0/a_1$ is replaced by its outward-rounded enclosure.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Other base cases]
The cascade only needs an enclosure of $Z_{p-1}$ as its starting point. Any certified solver for a polynomial of higher degree, such as closed-form roots bracketed with interval arithmetic, can replace the linear case, and the proof is unchanged with $p-1$ replaced by the starting derivative order.
\end{remark}


\section{Complexity analysis} \label{sec:complexity_analysis}
\paragraph*{Notation}
The trajectory has $N$ control points, degree $p$, $J$ joints, and $m=N-p$ segments. The local tolerance is $\varepsilon_\ell=m\,\varepsilon$. At cascade level $k$ the polynomial $f_k=P^{(k)}$ has degree $\delta=p-k\in\{2,\dots,p\}$. We count real or interval arithmetic operations, and an interval operation costs a constant factor more than a real one. Evaluating a polynomial of degree $\delta$ by Horner's scheme, or its derivative, costs $O(\delta)$. Let $n_k$ be the number of root enclosures held at level $k$, and let $r=\max_k n_k$ be the largest such number. Let $I$ be the maximal number of iterations of one call to \textsc{SafeNewton}.

\begin{proposition}[Time complexity]\label{prop:time}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:cascade} runs in
\begin{equation}
  T(N,p,J)=O\big(J\,(N-p)\,r\,I\,p^{2}\big)
\end{equation}
operations, and since $r\le p-1$ in the generic case below, $T=O\big(J\,(N-p)\,I\,p^{3}\big)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The segments and joints are processed independently, so the total cost is $J\,m$ times the cost for one polynomial $P$ of degree $p$. We count it step by step.

\emph{Local polynomial.} The coefficients of the segment are obtained as $A_i=M_iC_i$ with $M_i\in\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times(p+1)}$ and $C_i\in\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times J}$, which costs $O(p^2J)$ per segment, i.e., $O(p^2)$ per polynomial.

\emph{Derivatives.} Computing $f_1,\dots,f_{p-1}$ by repeated differentiation of the coefficient array costs $\sum_{\delta=2}^{p}O(\delta)=O(p^2)$.

\emph{Cascade level $k$ ($\delta=p-k$).} With $n_k\le r$ root enclosures at hand, \textsc{Refine} performs
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $n_k$ interval evaluations of $f_k$ for the retention test, and the same number again for recording candidates when $k\le d$, i.e., $O(n_k\delta)$;
  \item $O(n_k+1)$ point evaluations at the gap boundaries, i.e., $O((n_k+1)\delta)$;
  \item at most $n_k+1$ calls of \textsc{SafeNewton}, one per gap. Each call forms $f_k'$ in $O(\delta)$ and performs at most $I$ iterations, each with two Horner evaluations, which costs $O(I\delta)$.
\end{enumerate}
The Newton calls dominate, so the cost of level $k$ is $O\big((n_k+1)\,I\,\delta\big)=O(r\,I\,\delta)$.

\emph{Sum over levels.} $\sum_{\delta=2}^{p}O(r I\delta)=O(r\,I\,p^2)$. Adding the $O(p^2)$ terms above gives $O(r I p^2)$ per polynomial and $O(J\,m\,r\,I\,p^2)$ in total. Finally, $n_k\le\delta-1$ when each enclosure contains at most one real root of $f_{k+1}$, which gives $r\le p-1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Dependence on $N$, $p$, $J$]
For a fixed degree, the cost is linear in the number of control points and in the number of joints. In $p$ it is quadratic when the number of root enclosures per level is bounded by a constant (the typical case), and cubic in the worst case $r=p-1$. The segments and joints are independent and can be processed in parallel. The cost does not decrease with a smaller $d$, since the roots of $f_1,\dots,f_{d+1}$ are needed to report the extrema of all orders $0,\dots,d$. Only the candidate recording of order $k\le d$ depends on $d$, with a cost of $O(\delta\,n_k)$ per level, already included above.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Iterations of \textsc{SafeNewton}]
The count $I$ depends on $\varepsilon_\ell$. Pure bisection needs $\lceil\log_2(1/\varepsilon_\ell)\rceil$ iterations, and Newton steps near a simple root reduce this to a few iterations because of quadratic convergence. The implementation also bounds $I$ by a fixed cap $I_{\max}$, so $I\le I_{\max}$ always holds. Because the number of iterations needed for the bracket width to fall below $\varepsilon_\ell$ is not guaranteed to be logarithmic unless the Newton step is required to at least halve the previous step, we state the bound in terms of $I$, which is measured in the experiments.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Retained intervals]
The bound $r\le p-1$ assumes that every enclosure contains at most one root. An interval retained because $0\in\iv{f}(r)$ may contain no root, which can increase $n_k$ by at most the number of new real roots found at that level, so in general $n_k\le n_{k+1}+(p-k)$ and $r=O(p^2)$. The per-level cost is then $O(p^3)$ and the total per polynomial $O(p^4)$. Retention was never triggered in our experiments, so the bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:time} applies in practice.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Memory]
The working memory per segment is $O(p^2)$ for the derivative polynomials, plus $O(J\,m\,p)$ for the local polynomials. The output holds at most $n_k+2$ candidates per segment, joint and order $k\le d$, i.e., $O(J\,m\,d\,r)$ intervals, with $r\le p-1$.
\end{remark}


\section{Experimental Setup} \label{sec:experimental_setup}
We compare the proposed method against two baselines that also solve Problem~1: a
discretization-based method, which evaluates $q_j^{(k)}$ at $n_s=\mathrm{XX}$ uniformly spaced times
and takes the minimum and maximum of the samples, and a convex-hull-based method, which bounds
$q_j^{(k)}$ by the range of the control points of the derivative B-spline. All methods are applied
to derivative orders $k=0,\dots,d$ with $d=\mathrm{2}$, i.e. searching for extrema of position, velocity, and acceleration.

The evaluation uses two datasets of uniform, clamped B-splines (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}), chosen
to represent B-splines typically used in trajectory optimization. The proposed method is not
restricted to this setting: it applies to any B-spline with interior knots of multiplicity one and
$d\le p-2$. The randomized dataset contains $5\,600$ B-splines with $N\in\{12,14,\dots,26\}$ control
points and degrees $p\in\{4,5,\dots,10\}$. The optimized dataset contains $25\,600$ B-splines
generated by trajectory optimization with blast~\cite{XX}, all with $N=16$ control points and
degree $p=5$. Every B-spline is defined for a UR5e manipulator with $J=6$ joints, and each method
is applied to every joint independently to bound the extrema $q_{j,k}^{\min}$ and $q_{j,k}^{\max}$
of Problem~1.

\paragraph*{Reference and error metric}
Ground truth is obtained by a fine discretization: for every joint $j$ and derivative order
$k\in\{0,1,2\}$ (position, velocity, acceleration), $q_j^{(k)}$ is evaluated at $100\,000$ uniformly
spaced times over the trajectory duration, and the minimum and maximum of the samples give the
reference bounds $\underline{b}^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,k}$ and $\overline{b}^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,k}$. Because
the samples are a subset of the trajectory, these bounds lie within the true extrema.
To compare methods independently of the units and limits of each joint, each pair of bounds
$[\underline{b},\overline{b}]$ is converted into the normalized constraint value

\begin{equation}
  u_{j,k}=\frac{2}{w_{j,k}}\max\big\{\,|\underline{b}-c_{j,k}|,\ |\overline{b}-c_{j,k}|\,\big\},
\end{equation}
with the limit center $c_{j,k}=(\overline{L}_{j,k}+\underline{L}_{j,k})/2$ and width
$w_{j,k}=\overline{L}_{j,k}-\underline{L}_{j,k}$, where $[\underline{L}_{j,k},\overline{L}_{j,k}]$
are the limits of~\eqref{eq:limits}.

Here $u_{j,k}\le1$ means that the limits are respected and $u_{j,k}>1$ that they are violated. A trajectory is labelled
infeasible if $u^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,k}>1$ for some $j$ and $k$. The error of a method $\mathcal{M}$ is
reported as the fraction
\begin{equation}
  \rho^{\mathcal{M}}_{j,k}=\frac{u^{\mathcal{M}}_{j,k}}{u^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,k}},
\end{equation}
so that $\rho=1$ means agreement with the reference, $\rho<1$ means that the extrema are
underestimated, and $\rho>1$ means that they are overestimated. A sound method returns bounds that
contain the true extrema, hence $\rho\ge1$ for every $j$ and $k$. A violation is missed when
$u^{\mathcal{M}}_{j,k}\le1<u^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,k}$.

All experiments are run on a single thread of an XX CPU (XX cores, XX GHz base frequency, XX\,GB RAM)
under XX (operating system), with the code compiled with XX (compiler, version, optimization flags)
and double-precision arithmetic. Timings are measured with a monotonic wall-clock timer around the
complete evaluation of one B-spline for all $J$ joints, from the piecewise polynomial conversion to
the returned bounds. They exclude input and output and the computation of the reference. Each method
is run $15$ times on every B-spline, the first $5$ runs are discarded to remove cold-start effects
(instruction and data caches, branch predictors, and frequency scaling), and the reported time is
the mean of the remaining $10$ runs. Since all methods are deterministic, the returned bounds are
identical across runs, and the repetitions only reduce the measurement noise of the computation
time, not the variability of the accuracy results.

\section{Results and discussion}\label{sec:results_and_discussion}
We first study the influence of the tolerance $\varepsilon$ on accuracy and computation time
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation_epsilon}). The tolerance bounds the width of every root enclosure returned
by \textsc{SafeNewton}, measured as a fraction of the normalized trajectory duration, so that the
local tolerance on each segment is $m\,\varepsilon$ with $m=N-p$. We sweep
$\varepsilon\in\{\mathrm{XX}\}$ on the combined dataset, i.e., the union of the randomized and
optimized sets of Table~\ref{tab:datasets} ($31\,200$ B-splines), with derivative orders
$k=0,\dots,d$ and $d=\mathrm{XX}$. Accuracy is the relative overestimation $\rho-1$, where $\rho$
is the ratio of normalized constraint values defined in Section~\ref{sec:setup}, averaged over
joints, derivative orders and B-splines. Computation time is the mean wall-clock time per B-spline,
covering the conversion to piecewise polynomial form through the extrema of all $J$ joints and
excluding the reference computation. It is measured on a single thread of $\mathrm{XX}$
(hardware) and averaged over $\mathrm{XX}$ repetitions. By Theorem~\ref{thm:soundness}, the returned
bounds contain the true extrema for every $\varepsilon$, so $\rho\ge1$ throughout. Therefore
$\varepsilon$ affects only the tightness of the enclosures and the computation time.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{Figures/cascade_epsilon.pdf}
    \caption{Ablation on the tolerance $\varepsilon$ on the combined dataset. Top: mean relative
    overestimation $\rho-1$ with respect to the reference. Bottom: mean computation time per
    B-spline. Shaded regions show $\mathrm{XX}$. The horizontal axis and error axis are logarithmic.}
    \label{fig:ablation_epsilon}
\end{figure}

Decreasing $\varepsilon$ monotonically reduces the overestimation $\rho-1$, from XX at
$\varepsilon=10^{-1}$ to XX at $\varepsilon=10^{-7}$, while the computation time grows by a factor
of only XX over the same range. This weak dependence is consistent with the convergence of Newton's
method near simple roots: each additional decade of accuracy costs few extra iterations, whereas a
bisection-only search would need $\log_2 10\approx3.3$ more iterations per decade. Between
$\varepsilon=10^{-1}$ and $\varepsilon=10^{-2}$ the computation time decreases instead. A plausible
cause is that, for large $\varepsilon$, root enclosures remain wide enough for the interval
evaluation of the next derivative to contain zero, so they are retained rather than resolved. This
increases the number of enclosures per level and with it the number of interval and point
evaluations (Section~\ref{sec:complexity}). This is consistent with the mean number of enclosures
per level, XX at $\varepsilon=10^{-1}$ versus XX at $\varepsilon=10^{-2}$. At
$\varepsilon=10^{-7}$ the overestimation is XX, i.e., XX orders of magnitude below XX
(application-relevant reference, e.g., the constraint tolerance of the controller).

We then compare the proposed method with $\varepsilon=10^{-7}$ against three alternatives on the
same dataset and metrics (Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}): the convex-hull method, the discretization
method with $n_s$ uniformly spaced samples, and a variant of the proposed method in which the
Newton cascade is replaced by computing the real roots of $f_{k+1}$ in $[0,1]$ as eigenvalues of
its companion matrix, for each $k\le d$. The variant evaluates $f_k$ at these roots and at the
segment endpoints, and uses the same piecewise polynomial conversion. Hence, the comparison between
the variant and the convex-hull and discretization methods isolates the effect of the polynomial
decomposition, and the comparison between the cascade and the variant isolates the effect of the
Newton cascade as the root-finding step. The companion variant does not enclose the roots, so its
bounds are not certified and $\rho<1$ is possible. The convex-hull and companion variants do not
depend on $n_s$, which is the horizontal axis of Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison} and appears there
only as a constant line.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{Figures/collocation_n_points_ablation.pdf}
    \caption{Comparison of the methods on the combined dataset. Top: mean relative overestimation
    $\rho-1$ with respect to the reference. Bottom: mean computation time per B-spline. The
    horizontal axis is the number of samples $n_s$ of the discretization method. All other methods
    are independent of $n_s$ and are drawn as constant lines.}
    \label{fig:comparison}
\end{figure}


Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison} shows that neither baseline satisfies both requirements of
Problem~1. The convex-hull method is the fastest, XX times faster than the proposed method, but it
violates tightness: its mean overestimation is $83.4\%$ ($\rho\approx1.834$), and it rejects XX\%
of the trajectories that are feasible according to the reference. Its bounds depend only on the
control points of the derivative spline and not on the extrema actually attained, so they are
conservative by construction. The discretization method violates soundness. Its samples lie on
the trajectory, so it tends to underestimate the extrema ($\rho<1$) and can miss violations. At
$n_s=\mathrm{XX}$ it misses XX\% of the trajectories for which the reference is violated. The
missed-violation rate decreases with $n_s$, but no finite $n_s$ guarantees soundness, and a safety
margin that compensates for the underestimation cannot be certified without knowing the extrema.

The proposed method and the companion variant both reach a mean overestimation below $10^{-7}$.
They differ in their guarantees. The proposed method evaluates $f_k$ by interval arithmetic over
each root enclosure, so its bounds account for the remaining uncertainty on the root location and,
under the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:soundness}, contain the true extrema. The companion
variant evaluates $f_k$ at approximate roots obtained from an eigenvalue problem, whose accuracy
degrades for ill-conditioned polynomials, in particular with multiple or clustered roots, and it
may then yield $\rho<1$. We did not observe this: the minimum $\rho$ over the dataset is XX. The
proposed method is $2.4\times$ faster than the companion variant in mean computation time at
$\varepsilon=10^{-7}$. Its computation time matches that of the discretization method at
$n_s\approx360$, where the latter has a mean $\rho-1$ of XX and misses XX\% of the violations.
